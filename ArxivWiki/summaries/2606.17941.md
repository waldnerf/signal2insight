# PepsiCo Deploys AI-Driven Pricing and Promotion Optimization at Scale

arXiv: [2606.17941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.17941v1) &middot; Published 2026-06-16 &middot; Recommendation: **Priority review** (score 21/25)

_Full-text reassessed: screened on the complete PDF, not the abstract alone. Not yet through [[knowledge-extraction]]._

## High-Level Summary

PepsiCo built and deployed two production systems for Revenue Growth Management: PromoAI, which builds trade-promotion calendars, and PricingAI, which sets base prices. Both follow the same shape -- a machine learning model estimates demand, then a mathematical optimiser makes the actual decision under the business's own constraints. PromoAI runs a LightGBM demand forecast into a mixed-integer program solved by Gurobi, across hundreds of thousands of variables and constraints; PricingAI estimates own- and cross-price elasticities with a Bayesian hierarchical model and searches the resulting non-convex price landscape with differential evolution. PromoAI is live across 7+ countries and dozens of markets and supports 16 calendar refreshes a year; PricingAI runs in the US and Mexico over a 40+ product-group portfolio. About 85% of PromoAI's recommendations are accepted and executed by the business, and planning cycles that took weeks now take minutes. Published in INFORMS Journal on Applied Analytics.

## Key Highlights

- The paper argues directly that the optimiser, not the ML model, is where the business value sits. The forecast is an enabling input; without the optimisation layer the elasticity model would be 'a descriptive analytics tool' and the demand forecast would support only ad hoc manual planning.
- Adoption is reported as an outcome metric, not a footnote: ~85% of optimised promotional recommendations are accepted and executed in most markets, framed explicitly on the grounds that value is only realised when recommendations are actually implemented.
- Market rollout is a configuration step, not an engineering project. Business rules live in external JSON config files rather than in code, so a new market is onboarded by instantiating existing constraint modules with local parameters -- and business users change rules without a developer.
- The hard engineering is in making the problem solvable at all: the non-linear LightGBM demand curve is approximated by 2-4 piecewise-linear segments so it can enter a MILP, with breakpoint count chosen automatically by knee-point detection. Elasticities are decomposed across shared brand/size/flavour/retailer attributes to cut the parameter count below the number of price observations available.
- Limitations are stated plainly: MILP cannot express some non-linear business behaviour without workarounds, differential evolution gives no formally bounded optimality gap, and using posterior-mean elasticities discards the uncertainty the Bayesian model produced.
- The headline financial result is withheld. Senior management attests to measurable revenue and margin improvement, but the actual figures are commercially sensitive; the 2.0-5.0% figure the paper cites is an external industry benchmark, not PepsiCo's measured outcome.

## Why It Matters for Enterprise AI

This is a rare thing to read at executive level: a named Fortune 100 company describing what it actually took to run AI against a decision worth billions, with the organisational half of the story given equal weight to the technical half. Its most useful claim is a corrective one -- most enterprise AI programmes are pitched and budgeted around the model, while this one locates the value in the optimisation layer that turns a prediction into a decision, and treats the forecast as a component rather than the product. Its second most useful claim is that trust was the binding constraint, not accuracy: planners got interfaces that let them override a recommendation and immediately see the financial consequence, and the paper states outright that the organisational challenge of changing established workflows is at least as hard as building the models. For any leader weighing an AI investment in a planning-heavy function, the transferable content is the architecture pattern and the adoption playbook, not the CPG specifics.

## Business Applications

- Put an optimiser downstream of an existing forecasting model rather than expanding the model: keep the ML for demand estimation and let a solver make the constrained decision, which is also the more auditable half of the stack.
- Externalise business rules into configuration rather than code, so entering a new market or retailer is a config change owned by the business rather than a development cycle -- the single biggest lever the paper reports on cost of expansion.
- Track acceptance rate of recommendations as a first-class KPI for any decision-support deployment; it measures realised value in a way model accuracy does not.
- Design the override path deliberately: let planners change a recommendation and see the quantified downstream impact, which the paper identifies as the decisive factor in adoption across both systems.
- Expect data harmonisation, not algorithm design, to be the persistent cost in a multi-market rollout -- promotional flagging, price derivation and SKU matching all needed per-market calibration.
