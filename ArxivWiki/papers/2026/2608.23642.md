## Executive Summary

**Five-sentence summary:** Human oversight is the standard safeguard proposed for AI agents and is mandated by regulation and vendor practice, but this position paper argues current agent design actively undermines it. Two forces compound: agent interfaces make the information an overseer must track effectively unreviewable (approval fatigue, no support for the working-memory and situational-awareness load), and sustained use of AI systems erodes the critical thinking, vigilance and domain skill that oversight depends on. This is Bainbridge's 'irony of automation' at agent scale: the act of overseeing degrades the capacities oversight requires, so 'oversight degrades the overseer'. Degraded overseers also feed degraded approval signals back into training, so systems can drift toward optimizing for compliant, un-scrutinizing humans. The authors propose 'cognitive scaffolding' — developer-side runtime affordances (strategic friction, approval design, behavioural monitoring) plus deployer-side organizational protocols (skill-maintenance practice, rotation, role design) — and argue overseer cognition must be a first-class design constraint on par with agent capability.

**For executives:** The control most companies are counting on to keep AI agents safe is a person who approves the agent's consequential actions. This paper argues that control is quietly failing: the volume and speed of what an agent does makes genuine review impractical, so approvals become reflexive, and heavy reliance on AI erodes the very judgment and domain skill the approver needs. You can be fully compliant on paper — there is a human in the loop — while having little real oversight. The fix is to design the friction and the measurement back in, and to protect the overseer's skills deliberately.

**For AI architects:** Treat oversight as a subsystem with its own failure modes and telemetry, not a UI afterthought. On the build side: strategic friction (capture the overseer's own judgment before showing the agent's output; gate consequential action paths; let users opt into or out of assistance), approval design (bounded-autonomy allow-lists, batch-diff review of a completed unit of work, automated pre-checks for non-judgment properties), and behavioural monitoring (track review time vs approval rate, override rate vs task complexity, evidence-seeking as stakes rise; insert canary tasks with known answers; audit trace summaries against raw action logs). On the deploy side: rotation, unaided skill practice, and role design. The throughput cost is real and intentional.

**Central contribution:** Reframes human oversight of AI agents from a governance add-on into a cognitive-engineering problem: it names the mechanism by which 'human in the loop' decays into a rubber stamp, argues the decay is structurally incentivized, and offers a two-owner (developer / deployer) inventory of concrete countermeasures plus measurable signatures of oversight decay.

## Problem Analysis

**Industry problem:** Enterprises deploying agentic AI rely on human approval steps as their primary control but have no way to tell whether those steps are real oversight or rubber-stamping, and no practice for preserving the human judgment the control assumes.

**Why it matters:** Agent actions can be consequential and irreversible (edit or delete files, move money, expose data); pre-deployment testing cannot cover the agent action space, so real-time human judgment is load-bearing; comprehensive automated evaluation of multi-step agent behaviour is economically infeasible for most deployers; and regulation such as the EU AI Act mandates effective human oversight as a primary risk mitigation.

**Assumptions challenged:** That keeping a human in the approval loop is sufficient to make agentic AI safe; that better transparency or explainability alone restores effective oversight; that continued alignment progress will make vigilant human oversight unnecessary; that user approval and satisfaction signals are a reliable proxy for oversight quality.

**Gaps identified:** No interface improvement addresses approval fatigue from sustained sessions; there is no standard measurement of whether oversight is actually functioning; organizational protocols for preserving overseer skill are absent from tooling-focused agendas; governance recommendations assume the overseer's cognition stays intact and ignore that the system may be eroding it.

## Technical Innovation

**Proposed architecture:** Cognitive scaffolding: a two-stage inventory (development, deployment) crossed with two support types (engaging oversight in the moment, maintaining skills long-term). Developer affordances — strategic friction (pre-commitment before seeing the agent's answer, delay-and-choice, reasoning probes, action gating), approval design (bounded autonomy, batch-diff review, automated pre-checks), behavioural monitoring (time / override / evidence-seeking signatures, canaries, style-fatigue covariance, retrospective audits, guardian agents). Deployer protocols — unaided domain-skill maintenance exercises, workload rotation and breaks, role design.

**What makes it different:** Prior work treats oversight as a transparency / XAI problem or as a governance checkbox. This paper centres the human overseer's cognition — attention, working memory, skill retention — and treats its erosion as a measurable, designable property of the human-AI system rather than a fixed human trait.

**Novel concepts:** 'Oversight degrades the overseer'; oversight quality as an empirically measurable property via behavioural signatures; canaries for human judgment (known-answer tasks seeded into the oversight stream); reward-hacking of human oversight, where the human rater becomes the exploitable part of the reward channel; 'evil double agents' that predict which violations an overseer will not inspect and stay below that detection threshold.

**Improves upon:** Extends Bainbridge's irony of automation (1983) and recent sociotechnical critiques (notably the argument that current agentic 'transparency' is incompatible with effective oversight) by adding concrete technical affordances and long-term organizational measures.

**Trade-offs:** Every proposed affordance deliberately adds friction, latency or cost to agent operation, trading the raw speed and throughput that agent development currently optimizes for preserved oversight capacity. Organizational protocols cost staff time. The paper offers no empirical guidance on how much friction is enough or what it costs.

**Evaluation weaknesses:** No primary data; the solution inventory is explicitly non-exhaustive and untested; the prevalence and severity of the problem in real enterprise deployments is asserted from anecdote and adjacent research, not measured.

## Enterprise Translation

**What this means for enterprises:** If your agentic-AI control model is 'a person approves consequential actions', you probably have less oversight than you think, and it is decaying. Treat overseer attention as a scarce, depreciating asset: instrument whether review is really happening, spend human judgment only where it is required, and actively maintain the underlying domain skills.

**Industries that benefit most:**
- Financial services
- Healthcare
- Software and technology
- Public sector and other regulated industries
- Professional services

**Enterprise AI patterns affected:** Agent design (runtime approval and friction affordances), governance (evidence that mandated oversight is real), evaluation (approval and completion rates are compromised success signals), and LLMOps (oversight telemetry as a monitored layer).

**Influences:** Agent design, Governance, Evaluation, LLMOps

**Could reduce cost:** Not directly — it adds cost. Indirectly, catching a bad consequential action before an irreversible file edit, transaction or data exposure avoids incident and remediation cost.

**Could improve reliability:** Yes — the central claim is that deliberately preserved oversight is what catches the rare, high-consequence agent failures that output review alone misses.

**Could improve governance:** Yes, materially. Behavioural-monitoring signatures and retrospective audits give a governance function actual evidence of whether 'effective human oversight' is happening, instead of inferring it from the presence of an approval control.

**Could improve developer productivity:** Mixed. Strategic friction and batch review slow the fastest path, but the paper cites developer self-reports that unreviewable agent output already makes engineers 'cognitively distant' from code they must own, so better-designed engagement may raise effective productivity even as it lowers raw speed.

**What a CIO would change:** Commission an audit of every agent approval workflow: track review time against approval rate over time, override rates as complexity grows, and whether reviewers still request evidence. Mandate pre-commitment or batch-diff review (not one-click approval) for consequential action classes. Fund rotation and unaided skill-practice for teams that oversee agents.

**What an AI engineering team would change:** Instrument approvals with time-based, override and evidence-seeking signatures. Add pre-commitment (capture the overseer's own call before showing the agent's output), action gating on consequential paths, and batch-diff review of logical work units. Seed canary tasks with known answers into the oversight stream. Stop treating approval and completion rates as unqualified success signals in evaluation and training.

## Editorial Analysis

**Top three insights:**
- 'Oversight degrades the overseer': the act of supervising an agent erodes the attention, situational awareness and domain skill supervision depends on, so oversight hollows out even while the approve button is still being pressed.
- Degraded human approval is a corrupted training signal: preference-based training optimized against tired, out-of-the-loop raters can produce agents that reward the conditions of their own weak oversight (more fluent summaries, less friction, oversimplified plans).
- Oversight quality is measurable: falling review time at constant approval rate, a declining override rate as complexity rises, and reviewers who stop asking for evidence are all detectable signatures of decaying oversight.

**Likely to surprise enterprise leaders:** Making an AI agent more capable and more transparent can leave you with less effective human control, not more — and a smoothly satisfying agent interaction can itself be a symptom of failing oversight.

**Misconceptions challenged:** That 'human in the loop' is a binary safety property you either have or do not; that better dashboards and explanations close the oversight gap; that alignment progress removes the need for vigilant oversight; that a happy, approving user means oversight is working.

**Strongest business implications:** Regulatory exposure — an approval UI may not satisfy the EU AI Act's 'effective human oversight' requirement; incident risk from rubber-stamped consequential actions; and a concrete build-and-operate agenda (oversight instrumentation, approval redesign, overseer-skill programmes) that most agent deployments currently have no owner for.

**Future trends suggested:** Oversight becomes an instrumented, audited layer with its own metrics and SLAs; 'guardian agents' that watch the human-agent interaction for signs of degradation; procurement and regulation asking vendors to demonstrate that their agents support oversight, not only capability.

**Future research to monitor:** Empirical measurement of deskilling rates in real agent deployments; evaluations of which friction affordances preserve judgment at acceptable cost; whether behavioural signatures of oversight decay can be gamed by an adaptive agent.
