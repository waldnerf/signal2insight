## Executive Summary

**Five-sentence summary:** As LLM assistants acquire persistent memory, a failure mode emerges that recall benchmarks cannot see: the agent stores a user preference correctly and then does not act on it. This paper isolates that failure with a decoupled evaluation that administers paired Know and Act tests against the same preference, across 16 memory systems, five architecture families, and 1,000 preferences embedded at three levels of expression strength. The gap is pervasive: even the strongest system converts only about two-thirds of correctly remembered preferences into preference-aware behaviour, and the weakest can articulate a preference on request while almost never reflecting it in what it does. Utilisation is worst precisely where the stakes are highest, with health and medical preferences scoring lowest and therapy and emotional preferences second-lowest despite the latter having the highest recall. A controlled oracle study localises the breakdown: 53% of failures are comprehension (the right passage was in context but the constraint was not extracted), 31% retrieval, and 16% application, where the preference was stated outright and the model still did not act on it.

**For executives:** Memory is the feature every assistant vendor is selling right now, and it is being evaluated on the wrong thing. Every benchmark asks whether the system remembers. This paper asks the question that matters commercially: when it matters, does it act on what it remembers? Across sixteen systems the answer is often no. An assistant will correctly tell you it knows about your peanut allergy and then recommend a Thai dish. The gap is widest in health and emotional contexts, which is exactly where a failure to act has consequences rather than being merely annoying. For anyone deploying a memory-enabled assistant to customers or employees, this establishes that recall accuracy in a vendor demo tells you almost nothing about behaviour in production, and gives a testing method that does.

**For AI architects:** The design is the contribution: for each user preference, generate a paired Know test (a direct first-person recall question) and an Act test (a realistic request where the preference should shape the response but is never mentioned). Act is scored by paired comparison against the same backbone with no memory, judged binary by GPT-5, which controls for the model's baseline behaviour rather than measuring absolute quality. Utilisation Rate is then the fraction of correctly recalled preferences the agent also acts on. Preferences are embedded at three expression strengths (Explicit, Incidental, Inferential) with identical non-target distractor chunks across conditions so only the target snippet varies. Sixteen systems span long-context baselines, simple and embedding RAG, structure-augmented RAG and agentic memory, with all non-long-context systems sharing GPT-4o-mini as backbone so architecture is isolated from generation capability. The failure attribution uses two oracle conditions (inject the ground-truth chunk; inject the preference text itself) to partition failures into retrieval, comprehension and application.

**Central contribution:** A decoupled Know/Act evaluation paradigm that separates memory failure from utilisation failure, the finding that the utilisation gap is large and pervasive across all memory architectures, and a three-layer oracle attribution showing comprehension rather than retrieval is the dominant cause.

## Problem Analysis

**Industry problem:** Memory-augmented assistants are being deployed as long-term companions and enterprise agents, evaluated on benchmarks that test factual recall, multi-hop reasoning and long-range understanding. None of those detect the case where the system retrieves the right information and still behaves as if it had not, which is the failure users actually experience and the one that carries risk.

**Why it matters:** It is a measurement failure with a safety edge. When an agent fails to tailor its response, the organisation cannot currently tell whether the model forgot or remembered and ignored, and those have completely different fixes. Worse, the gap concentrates in health, medical and emotional preferences, where the paper argues failure to act carries tangible risk. Any organisation buying or building on memory features is currently making that purchase against metrics that do not measure the behaviour it is buying.

**Assumptions challenged:** That retrieval accuracy is a proxy for personalised behaviour. That better memory architectures solve personalisation, when they narrow but do not close the gap. That the intuitive difficulty ordering holds, since Incidental preferences buried as side details proved harder to recall than Inferential ones requiring reasoning from behavioural cues. And the assumption that failures are retrieval problems, when comprehension dominates at 53%.

**Gaps identified:** Existing memory benchmarks conflate storage with utilisation and therefore cannot localise a failure. No prior work paired a recall probe with a behavioural probe on the same item. Expression strength was not treated as an experimental variable, so the field could not see that explicit statement exposes utilisation failure while non-explicit expression exposes storage failure. And no prior study decomposed memory-pipeline failures into retrieval, comprehension and application layers.

## Technical Innovation

**Proposed architecture:** An evaluation framework rather than a system. Inject-then-query protocol following MemoryAgentBench: conversation chunks are fed one at a time through each system's native memorisation mechanism, then Know and Act tests are administered in separate independent sessions to avoid priming. 150 chunk sequences over 50 personas and three expression types, 11,955 chunks, 4.55M tokens, with dependency-aware ordering and non-target distractors including ask-to-forget entries and sensitive-information entries. A zero-memory baseline validates that Know questions are unanswerable without memory (all models under 3%). Two oracle conditions progressively bypass retrieval and then comprehension to attribute failures.

**What makes it different:** Prior memory benchmarks (LongMemEval, MemoryAgentBench, PersonaMem) measure whether information can be recovered. This measures whether recovered information changes behaviour, on the same item, which is what makes the dissociation visible at all. The paired-comparison Act judge against a zero-memory response from the same backbone is a better control than absolute scoring. And the oracle decomposition is the part most reusable elsewhere: it turns an aggregate failure rate into an actionable diagnosis.

**Novel concepts:** The Know/Act dissociation; Utilisation Rate as a metric distinct from recall accuracy; expression strength (Explicit, Incidental, Inferential) as a controlled experimental dimension; and three-layer failure attribution via progressive oracle injection.

**Improves upon:** LongMemEval, MemoryAgentBench and PersonaMem-v1/v2 on the evaluation side, all of which measure recall. It builds directly on PersonaMem-v2 for persona and preference material while adding the behavioural probe those benchmarks lack.

**Trade-offs:** The design buys clean attribution at the cost of realism, and the authors say so: each preference maps to a single target passage with limited competing evidence, which makes retrieval far easier than in production, so the observed gap is a conservative lower bound. Everything rests on LLM judges, mitigated by strong agreement statistics but not eliminated. Personas are synthetic. And using GPT-4o-mini as the shared backbone isolates architecture cleanly but means absolute numbers for the RAG and agentic systems understate what they would do on a stronger model.

## Evidence

**Evaluation method:** 16 memory systems across five architecture categories, 1,000 preferences per system, three expression conditions, paired Know and Act tests per preference in independent sessions. Know scored against structured ground truth; Act scored by binary paired comparison against a zero-memory response from the same backbone, judged by GPT-5. Zero-memory baseline confirms Know questions are unguessable (all models below 3%, anti-stereotypical lowest at 1.2%). Judge reliability established by human evaluation of 100 Know and 100 Act judgments (Fleiss kappa 0.89 and 0.82) and cross-judge agreement between GPT-5 and Claude Opus 4.6 on 200 samples (Cohen kappa 0.91 and 0.88). Failure attribution via oracle-chunk and oracle-preference injection over all 722 Know-Pass/Act-Fail cases for Mem0.

**Datasets:**
- KnowAct benchmark (1,000 preferences, 50 personas, 150 chunk sequences, 11,955 chunks, 4.55M tokens)
- PersonaMem-v2 (source personas and preferences)

**Benchmarks:**
- KnowAct (introduced here)
- MemoryAgentBench (inject-then-query protocol)
- PersonaMem-v2

**Statistically convincing?** Yes, and unusually well controlled for an LLM-judged benchmark. The zero-memory baseline is the right validity check and it passes decisively (under 3% across all four generation models), so non-trivial Know accuracy genuinely reflects memorisation. Judge reliability is established two ways, with human agreement at Fleiss kappa 0.89/0.82 and cross-model agreement at Cohen kappa 0.91/0.88. Holding the backbone constant across all non-long-context systems isolates architecture from generation capability, which most comparative memory studies fail to do. Sample sizes are large (1,000 preferences, 722 attributed failure cases). The main caveat is not statistical but constructional: the retrieval task is deliberately easy, which the authors correctly flag as making the reported gap a lower bound.

**Evaluation weaknesses:** Synthetic personas rather than real longitudinal user histories, so no natural topic drift or contradiction. Single target passage per preference with limited distractors, which simplifies retrieval well below production conditions. Total reliance on LLM judges for both tests, with acknowledged risks of verbosity preference and insensitivity to subtle behavioural cues. Therapy and emotional preferences carry the highest zero-memory pass rate at 9.0%, meaning empathetic defaults inflate Act scores in exactly the category the paper highlights. And the failure attribution is run on a single system (Mem0), so the 53/31/16 split may not generalise across architectures.

**Open questions:** How wide the gap becomes under realistic retrieval noise, contradiction and topic drift. Whether the comprehension bottleneck is a backbone-capability limit or a context-presentation problem, given that memory architectures improved utilisation more than storage. Whether the application-failure floor (stable at roughly 14-19% regardless of expression) is an irreducible property of current models. And whether the health and therapy utilisation gap persists with domain-specialised models.

## Enterprise Translation

**What this means for enterprises:** If you are deploying or procuring a memory-enabled assistant, your acceptance criteria are almost certainly wrong. Recall accuracy is the metric vendors report and it is close to uninformative about behaviour: systems here score 99% on recall and convert only 30-65% of that into action. The immediate practical move is to build paired tests, one asking the system what it knows and one putting it in a situation where knowing should change what it does, and to measure the ratio between them. Second, the risk is not evenly distributed: health and emotional contexts show the largest gaps, so any deployment touching wellbeing, benefits, care or HR needs that category tested separately rather than trusting an aggregate. Third, the finding that memory architectures improve utilisation more than storage suggests that presenting information in an actionable form matters more than retrieving more of it.

**Industries that benefit most:**
- Healthcare and digital health
- Financial services and wealth advisory
- Retail and e-commerce personalisation
- Human resources and employee experience
- Education and training
- Customer service across all sectors
- Insurance

**Enterprise AI patterns affected:** Memory and personalisation architecture directly; agent evaluation practice, since the Know/Act split is a general template for any capability where possession of information is distinct from acting on it; RAG design, since the comprehension-dominant failure mode argues for how retrieved context is presented rather than how much is retrieved; and AI governance for wellbeing-adjacent deployments, where the safety argument here is concrete.

**Influences:** Memory, RAG, Agent design, Evaluation, Governance

**Could reduce cost:** Not directly, and there is a counter-signal worth noting: the strongest results came from 1M-token long-context models, which is the expensive path. The cost-relevant finding is that Mem0 roughly tripled the Utilisation Rate of its GPT-4o-mini backbone to reach parity with Gemini 3.1 Flash, meaning architecture can substitute for context length and model tier on this task.

**Could improve reliability:** It measures reliability rather than improving it, which is the point. The Know/Act ratio is a reliability metric for personalisation that did not previously exist, and the three-layer attribution turns a reliability complaint into a specific engineering target.

**Could improve governance:** Yes, materially. It gives a defensible testing protocol for personalised systems and identifies a risk concentration (health, medical, therapy) that a governance framework can name and test for specifically. The finding that memory-augmented systems override demographic priors while the bare backbone falls back on them is also directly relevant to bias review.

**Could improve developer productivity:** Indirectly. The oracle-based attribution method means teams can stop guessing whether a personalisation complaint is a retrieval, comprehension or application problem, which is the diagnostic step that otherwise consumes the most time.

**What a CIO would change:** Change your acceptance criteria for anything with a memory feature. Require the vendor or your own team to report a utilisation rate, not a recall rate: given that the system correctly remembers something, how often does it act on it. Test health, medical and emotional categories separately rather than in aggregate, because that is where the gap concentrates and where the consequences are real. And treat a demo in which the assistant correctly answers what do you know about me as evidence of nothing.

**What an AI engineering team would change:** Build paired Know/Act evaluation sets for your own domain preferences and track the ratio as a first-class metric. Adopt the oracle-injection method to localise failures before optimising anything, since comprehension dominates and retrieval tuning will not fix it. Focus on how retrieved context is presented to the generator, not only on retrieval quality, given that memory architectures improved utilisation more than storage. And test incidentally expressed preferences deliberately, since those proved hardest to store despite intuition suggesting the opposite.

## Editorial Analysis

**Top three insights:**
- Remembering and acting are different capabilities and the gap between them is large: systems reach 99% recall accuracy while converting only 30-65% of correctly remembered preferences into preference-aware behaviour, and the best system converts only about two-thirds.
- The dominant cause is comprehension, not retrieval. In a controlled oracle study, 53% of failures occurred with the correct passage already in context, 31% were retrieval, and 16% persisted even when the preference was injected as plain text, revealing a floor in the model's ability to act on information it has been handed outright.
- The utilisation gap is largest exactly where the stakes are highest: health and medical preferences score lowest on utilisation and therapy and emotional preferences second-lowest, despite therapy preferences having the highest recall accuracy of any category.

**Likely to surprise enterprise leaders:** That in 16% of failures, the model was handed the preference as an explicit statement in its context (this user has a pollen allergy) and still did not act on it. No retrieval, no comprehension, no memory architecture fixes that, and the proportion stays remarkably stable regardless of how the preference was originally expressed.

**Misconceptions challenged:** That memory benchmarks measure personalisation. That a system which can tell you what it knows about you will use it. That better retrieval is the lever for better personalisation, when comprehension dominates failures. That subtly expressed preferences are harder than inferred ones, when Incidental proved harder to store than Inferential. And that adding a memory architecture solves the problem, when it narrows the gap without closing it.

**Strongest business implications:** Every organisation buying assistant memory is buying it against a metric that does not measure the behaviour they want. That is a procurement and acceptance-testing problem with an immediate fix. Beyond that, the concentration of failures in health and emotional contexts creates a real deployment constraint for any consumer or employee-facing assistant in wellbeing-adjacent domains, and the paper supplies both the argument and the test. For product teams, the finding that presentation of retrieved context matters more than volume of retrieval redirects effort away from the most commonly optimised component.

**Future trends suggested:** Evaluation moving from possession of information to use of information across agent capabilities generally, not just memory. Utilisation-style metrics appearing alongside recall in vendor reporting. Memory architectures optimised for actionable presentation rather than retrieval coverage. And a likely regulatory or procurement interest in behavioural testing for personalised systems in health-adjacent contexts.

**Future research to monitor:** Evaluation on real longitudinal user histories with natural drift and contradiction, which the authors flag as the key validity extension. Whether the application-failure floor is reducible through training rather than architecture. Extension of the oracle-attribution method to other agent pipelines. HaluMem and LifeBench as adjacent memory-evaluation lines, and BenchPres on context-aware preference selectivity.

- Anti-stereotypical preference

**Reusable architectural patterns:**
- Paired probes on the same item to separate two capabilities that aggregate metrics conflate
- Paired-comparison judging against a no-capability baseline from the same backbone, rather than absolute scoring
- Progressive oracle injection to localise a failure within a multi-stage pipeline
- Holding the generation backbone constant across systems to isolate architectural contribution
- Controlled distractor sets identical across experimental conditions so only the manipulated variable differs

**Evaluation methods worth remembering:**
- Decoupled Know/Act paired testing
- Zero-memory baseline validation that questions are unanswerable without the capability under test
- Dual judge-reliability establishment via human agreement and cross-model agreement
- Oracle-condition failure attribution
- Category-stratified analysis to expose risk concentration hidden by aggregate scores
