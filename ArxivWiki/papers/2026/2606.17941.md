## Executive Summary

**Five-sentence summary:** PepsiCo built and deployed two large-scale optimisation systems for revenue growth management: PromoAI for promotional calendars and PricingAI for base pricing. PromoAI couples a LightGBM demand forecast with a mixed-integer linear program, navigating millions of product-promotion-timing combinations under slotting, exclusivity, spacing and margin constraints, and now runs across seven-plus countries supporting 16 calendar refreshes a year. PricingAI estimates own and cross-price elasticities with a Bayesian hierarchical model decomposed across brand, size, flavour and retailer, then feeds them into a differential-evolution optimiser handling price ladders and psychological thresholds, deployed in the US and Mexico. About 85% of PromoAI recommendations were accepted and executed by the business, and planning cycles for specific tasks compressed from weeks to minutes. The authors are explicit that the decisive factor was not algorithmic: giving planners the ability to override a recommendation and immediately see the quantified consequence is what built organisational trust, and they state that this organisational challenge was at least as hard as the modelling one.

**For executives:** This is what a genuinely deployed enterprise AI system looks like once the pilot phase is over, published with numbers rather than claims. Two systems, running in production across continents, doing decisions that used to be made in spreadsheets by experienced people. The headline result is not the optimiser: it is that 85% of the machine's recommendations were actually executed, which is the only number that matters because value is realised on implementation, not on generation. Equally important is how they got there. The system was designed so planners could change any recommendation and instantly see what it cost them, which turned it from a black box into a negotiating instrument they took into meetings with retailers. The paper says plainly that winning that trust was as hard as building the models, which is the part most AI programmes underestimate.

**For AI architects:** Two decoupled stacks on shared Azure infrastructure: a web UI submits JSON scenario configurations through Azure Data Factory to Databricks notebooks that execute the optimisation. PromoAI is a MILP solved with Gurobi 12, using a piecewise linear approximation of the LightGBM demand forecast to convert a non-convex problem into a solvable one, with SKUs aggregated into Product Promotion Groups for dimensionality reduction, callback-based early termination on MILP gap stagnation, and market-specific target gaps of 1-5%. Runtimes range from minutes to 7-9 hours at 90-plus PPGs over 52 weeks. PricingAI estimates elasticities in STAN via ADVI with additive decomposition for own-price and multiplicative for cross-price, then runs up to 10 seeded differential-evolution instances in parallel to mitigate local optima, typically converging in under 30 minutes. The architectural point worth stealing is the decoupling discipline: ML models versioned separately from optimisers, and all business rules externalised into JSON configuration so a new market is onboarded by writing a config file rather than by touching code.

**Central contribution:** A documented, at-scale enterprise deployment of ML-plus-mathematical-optimisation for pricing and promotion across multiple markets, with adoption and impact numbers, and an explicit account of the human-in-the-loop design and organisational trust work that made the adoption happen.

## Problem Analysis

**Industry problem:** Pricing and promotion planning in consumer packaged goods requires simultaneously reasoning about demand elasticity, competitor actions, retailer constraints, cannibalisation across a portfolio, price ladders and psychological thresholds. Manual and heuristic approaches, however experienced, cannot navigate a solution space of millions of product-promotion-timing combinations, and every market historically used its own methodology, blocking any centralised or comparable approach.

**Why it matters:** For a company with revenue above $90 billion, pricing and promotion is the primary revenue lever, and the interdependency structure makes it exactly the class of problem humans reason about badly: a fractional price change on one product ripples through substitution and complementarity across the whole assortment simultaneously. The generic importance is broader than CPG: this is the archetype of a high-stakes recurring commercial decision where the analytics were already good and the bottleneck was turning prediction into prescription and getting people to act on it.

**Assumptions challenged:** That better forecasting is what unlocks value, when the paper argues forecasting without optimisation leaves planners with better information and no mechanism to act on it at scale. That deployment is an engineering project with an end date, when heterogeneous retailer data made it an ongoing per-market adaptation. That model accuracy drives adoption, when the decisive factor was override transparency. And that optimisation output should be presented as a recommendation to accept or reject, when presenting a menu of scenarios for commercial negotiation is what made it usable.

**Gaps identified:** The absence of a unified methodological framework across global markets, which blocked consistency and centralised optimisation. The gap between descriptive elasticity analytics and prescriptive pricing decisions. And the gap between commercially sound recommendations and operationally feasible ones, since retailers have planning cycles, shelf constraints and vendor commitments the model does not encode.

## Technical Innovation

**Proposed architecture:** PromoAI: LightGBM lift forecasting, piecewise-linearised into a MILP over binary promotion decisions with hundreds of thousands of variables and constraints, solved by Gurobi with market-specific gap targets and stagnation callbacks. PricingAI: Bayesian hierarchical elasticity estimation in STAN via ADVI, with own-price elasticity decomposed additively across grand, brand, size, taste and banner components and cross-price decomposed multiplicatively, conjoint survey data supplying informative priors where available and a softmax penalty enforcing sign consistency, feeding a differential-evolution optimiser run as up to 10 seeded parallel instances. Both share a decoupled Azure and Databricks architecture with JSON-externalised business rules and interactive scenario UIs.

**What makes it different:** Against typical published optimisation work, the differentiator is that this ran in production across continents rather than in a case study, and reports adoption rather than only solution quality. Against typical enterprise AI reporting, the differentiator is candour: it names the approximations that made the problem tractable and the limitations they introduce, and it reports the organisational failure modes rather than only the wins. The elasticity decomposition into shared attribute components is a genuinely useful modelling answer to sparse SKU-level data.

**Novel concepts:** Hierarchical attribute-based elasticity decomposition as an identification strategy under sparsity; JSON-externalised constraint modules as the unit of market onboarding; the adjusted-scenario pattern where an override displays its full portfolio-wide financial consequence rather than simply replacing the recommendation; and sequential prior updating across planning cycles to prevent destabilising swings in elasticity estimates between refreshes.

**Improves upon:** Manual, spreadsheet-driven planning based on domain expertise and historical heuristics, and market-by-market bespoke methodologies. It positions itself as an extension to those methods rather than a replacement, which is consistent with how it was actually adopted.

**Trade-offs:** Every tractability decision costs something and the authors say so. The piecewise linear approximation of the LightGBM forecast turns days into hours but means the MILP cannot express certain nonlinear business behaviours without workarounds. Aggregating SKUs into PPGs reduces dimensionality but loses SKU-level resolution. Differential evolution handles the non-convex pricing landscape but gives no bounded optimality gap, unlike the MILP. Using posterior mean elasticities rather than full distributions keeps optimisation tractable but discards uncertainty. And the human-in-the-loop workflow that drove adoption is also slower than autonomous execution.

## Evidence

**Evaluation method:** Production deployment rather than controlled experiment. Forecasting evaluated by rolling-origin backtesting with MAPE at SKU-retailer-week level against legacy benchmarks. Elasticities validated in three stages: in-sample fit thresholds (R-squared above 0.6, wMAPE below 0.4, absolute bias under 10%), business validation of magnitudes at brand-unit aggregation with own-price elasticities expected in the -2.5 to 0 range, and end-to-end pipeline testing on the validated matrix. Optimisation verified by confirming constraint satisfaction on historical and simulated scenarios plus sensitivity analysis. Impact reported as adoption metrics from live use.

**Datasets:**
- PepsiCo internal historical sales, pricing and promotional data across markets
- Retailer point-of-sale and syndicated CPG retail data
- Conjoint survey data used as informative priors for elasticity components

**Benchmarks:**
- Legacy internal forecasting benchmarks (MAPE at SKU-retailer-week level)
- Historical planner decisions used as a comparison baseline in scenario review

**Statistically convincing?** Partly, and the paper is honest about which part. The technical validation protocol is solid and specific, with pre-declared acceptance thresholds and economic-consistency checks on elasticity signs and magnitudes. The impact claims are a different matter: 85% recommendation acceptance and weeks-to-minutes cycle compression are adoption and efficiency metrics, not causal revenue attribution, and there is no control market or holdout showing what revenue would have been without the systems. That is a normal limitation of published industrial deployments and it does not undermine the operational claims, but it means this evidences feasibility and adoption rather than quantified financial uplift.

**Evaluation weaknesses:** No counterfactual for business impact: nothing establishes what would have happened without the systems. Deployment breadth differs sharply between the two systems (PromoAI in 7-plus countries, PricingAI in 2), so PricingAI's evidence base is thinner than the paper's joint framing suggests. Differential evolution offers no bounded optimality gap, so PricingAI's solution quality is not formally characterised. Adoption metrics are reported without denominators in places. And the whole account is single-company, with data that cannot be independently examined.

**Open questions:** Whether the 85% acceptance rate holds as the systems expand into markets with poorer data. Whether the elasticity decomposition generalises to portfolios with different attribute structures. What the actual revenue and margin effect is, as distinct from the planning efficiency effect. And whether the human-in-the-loop workflow is a permanent design requirement or a transitional trust mechanism.

## Enterprise Translation

**What this means for enterprises:** This is a reference implementation for the class of AI investment that most reliably returns: not autonomous decision-making but constraint-respecting decision support on a recurring, high-value commercial process. Three things transfer directly. First, the value came from the optimisation layer, not the prediction layer, which reverses where most enterprise AI budget currently goes. Second, externalising business rules into configuration turned market expansion from an engineering project into a configuration exercise, which is the difference between a system deployed in one place and one deployed in seven. Third, and most quotable, the paper states that gaining organisational trust and changing established workflows was at least as difficult as building the models, and that deployment strategies which underestimate this fail regardless of algorithmic quality.

**Industries that benefit most:**
- Consumer packaged goods
- Retail and grocery
- Manufacturing and distribution
- Airlines, hospitality and any revenue-management-driven business
- Telecommunications and subscription pricing
- Energy and commodity trading

**Enterprise AI patterns affected:** Decision-support architecture generally: the separation of forecasting from optimisation, versioned independently. Configuration-driven multi-market rollout. Human-in-the-loop override design where the override displays its own cost. And the operating-model question of whether AI systems are positioned as autonomous decision-makers or as instruments people carry into negotiations.

**Influences:** Governance, Infrastructure, LLMOps

**Could reduce cost:** Indirectly but materially. Planning and execution cycles for specific pricing and promotional tasks compressed from weeks to minutes, and markets scaled usage across more retailers and categories without proportional increases in planning effort or headcount. The paper frames this as lowering the barrier to adoption in new geographies rather than as headcount reduction, which is the more honest framing.

**Could improve reliability:** Yes, in the operational sense. The optimiser guarantees constraint satisfaction across hundreds of thousands of business rules that manual planning could only approximate, with bounded optimality gaps of 1-5% on the MILP side. Seeded runs make PricingAI reproducible, which the authors note matters for business coherence as much as for debugging. Sequential prior updating prevents destabilising elasticity swings between planning cycles.

**Could improve governance:** Yes, in a specific and underrated way: encoding business rules as externalised JSON configuration makes commercial policy explicit, inspectable and versioned rather than resident in individual planners' judgement. It also creates a consistent methodological framework across markets that previously each used their own approach, which is a comparability and control benefit before it is an optimisation one.

**Could improve developer productivity:** Not the paper's focus, but the decoupling discipline is the relevant lesson: separating ML model training and versioning from the optimisation engine let data science iterate on predictions without destabilising the optimiser, and JSON-externalised rules let business users change constraints without developer involvement. That last point removes a standing queue of small change requests from the engineering team.

**What a CIO would change:** Ask where your analytics investment actually sits on the predict-versus-prescribe line. This paper argues, with a deployed system behind it, that forecasting without an optimisation layer leaves people better informed and no more able to act at scale. Then look at your deployed AI systems and ask whether a user can override a recommendation and immediately see the quantified consequence: that specific capability, not model accuracy, is what this team credits for adoption. Finally, budget deployment as continuous per-market adaptation rather than as a project with a completion date.

**What an AI engineering team would change:** Decouple prediction from optimisation and version them separately. Externalise business rules into configuration rather than embedding them in code, and treat a new market or business unit as a config file. Where per-entity data is too sparse for per-entity parameters, decompose into shared attribute components rather than accepting noisy individual estimates. Build the override-with-consequences view before building the recommendation view. And be explicit internally about which approximations were made for tractability and what they cost.

## Editorial Analysis

**Top three insights:**
- The value came from the optimisation layer, not the forecasting layer. The authors state it directly: forecasting without optimisation leaves planners with better information but no mechanism to translate it into decisions at scale, and it is the optimiser that converts predictive analytics into prescriptive value.
- About 85% of the system's recommendations were accepted and executed, and the team attributes that to override transparency rather than model accuracy. Planners could change any recommendation and immediately see the portfolio-wide financial consequence, which turned the system from a black box into an instrument they used in retailer negotiations.
- Externalising all business rules into JSON configuration rather than code made market expansion a configuration exercise instead of an engineering project, which is the concrete mechanism behind deployment across seven-plus countries with widely varying market structures.

**Likely to surprise enterprise leaders:** That the organisational challenge of gaining trust and changing established workflows was at least as difficult as the technical challenge of building the models, and that deployment strategies underestimating this tend to fail regardless of algorithmic quality. Stated plainly, in a paper from a team that clearly could have chosen to talk about their solver instead.

**Misconceptions challenged:** That the hard part of enterprise AI is the model. That better forecasts automatically produce better decisions. That deployment is a finite engineering project rather than continuous per-market adaptation. That autonomy is the goal, when the systems that got adopted were explicitly positioned as augmenting rather than replacing judgement. And that approximations made for tractability are embarrassing details rather than the engineering content.

**Strongest business implications:** For any organisation with a recurring, constraint-heavy commercial decision, this establishes that the prescriptive layer is where the return is and that it is achievable at global scale with existing tooling. It also reframes the adoption problem: the deliverable is not a recommendation, it is a negotiating instrument that shows the cost of departing from it. Commercially, the compression of planning cycles from weeks to minutes changes what a planning function can attempt, allowing scenario exploration inside a single working session rather than across iterations, which is a capability change rather than an efficiency gain.

**Future trends suggested:** Optimisation returning to prominence as the layer that makes machine learning actionable, particularly as LLM interfaces make constraint specification accessible to non-technical users. Configuration-driven multi-market deployment becoming the standard pattern for global rollouts of decision systems. And a shift in how enterprise AI success is reported, from model metrics toward recommendation acceptance rates, which is the metric that actually tracks realised value.

**Future research to monitor:** Work bridging LLM interfaces with classical optimisation solvers, which is the natural next step for constraint specification. Uncertainty-aware optimisation that carries full posterior distributions rather than posterior means without losing tractability. And research on the determinants of recommendation acceptance in decision-support systems, which this paper identifies as decisive but only characterises anecdotally.

**Reusable architectural patterns:**
- Decoupled UI, optimisation engine and data layers allowing independent scaling and upgrade
- ML models trained and versioned separately from the optimisation engine
- Business rules externalised as configuration rather than embedded in code
- Piecewise linearisation of a nonlinear learned model to make it solvable inside a MILP
- Parallel seeded metaheuristic runs with varied hyperparameters to mitigate local optima
- Human-in-the-loop scenario interface where manual overrides display their quantified cost

**Evaluation methods worth remembering:**
- Rolling-origin backtesting for time-series forecast validation
- Three-stage validation combining statistical fit thresholds, business plausibility of parameter magnitudes, and end-to-end pipeline testing
- Economic consistency checks on learned parameters (sign and magnitude bounds)
- Constraint-satisfaction verification against historical and simulated scenarios
- Recommendation acceptance rate as the primary adoption metric
- Monitoring user override behaviour during pilot to identify model adjustments
